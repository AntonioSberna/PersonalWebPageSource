Hardly anyone who worked the fields ever felt the need for a gym. We go to the gym because our jobs stopped asking anything of our bodies: machines took over the effort, and we had to invent a place where we could put it back on purpose. The Greeks, who gave us the word, already knew the pattern. Their gymnasia were meant for free citizens, while slaves, who did most of the work with their hands, were [barred by law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_(ancient_Greece)).

I have been thinking about this because something similar seems to be happening to the other half of us.



## The hands of the intellect

In a [previous post](../2026-ecee-science-w-agents/) I ended with a weaver who adapted to the power loom and still knew what cloth was for. Every wave of mechanisation has worked like that: it took over the hands of a trade and left standing whoever understood what the trade was for. The power loom took the hands of the weavers, the steam engine the muscles of men and horses, the electronic computer the pencils of the human computers. (*Computer* was a job title long before it was a machine. *Agent* seems to be going the same way.)

The human computers deserve a moment. During the First World War, while serving with an ambulance unit on the Western Front, [Lewis Fry Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Fry_Richardson) spent the best part of six weeks computing by hand a six-hour weather forecast for a single point in central Europe. He then imagined how to compute faster than the weather itself: a hall like a theatre, 64,000 people with pencils and slide rules, and in the middle a man keeping them all in step, like the conductor of [_"an orchestra in which the instruments are slide-rules and calculating machines."_](https://www.emetsoc.org/resources/rff/) The theatre was never built. In 1950 the first computer forecast ran on ENIAC, and the 64,000 players were no longer needed.

Today that theatre is being rebuilt, this time filled with agents. In that same post I mentioned 10,000 of them working in parallel on Navier-Stokes. They read the literature, write the code, run the analyses and draft the papers. If they are becoming the workforce of the intellectual professions, what is left for us is the job of the man in the middle.



## What the conductor knows

In 1843 Ada Lovelace wrote that Babbage's Analytical Engine, which borrowed its punched cards from the Jacquard loom, [_"has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform."_](https://blogs.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/adalovelace/2018/07/26/ada-lovelace-and-the-analytical-engine/) The first half has aged badly (Turing was already [arguing with it](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433) in 1950, under the heading *Lady Lovelace's Objection*). The second half has become a job description.

A conductor does not need to know how to play the oboe. He needs to know what the oboe can do: its range, how long it can sustain a phrase before running out of breath, how it blends with the clarinets, and that the whole orchestra tunes to its A. He does not know the fingerings; he knows the score, and he hears when something is off. This is what I mean by theory. Not the production of new theorems, which agents now seem able to manage on their own, but having in one's head the model that tells a plausible result from a wrong one.

Agents are a superb orchestra: they play whatever is on the stand, wrong notes included, with exactly the same confidence. Without theory, you cannot hear the difference.



## The gym

LLMs may be the best gym for theory ever built. Every exchange forces you to say exactly what you want and to judge what comes back. You can ask for the same derivation five times in five different ways, request a counterexample at two in the morning, or argue about a sign convention until you finally see why it is the way it is (something I [wrote about](../2026-on-thinking-differently-screen/) recently). No professor has ever had that much patience.

But a gym is full of machines, and none of them lifts the weights for you. The tool that can train your theory can just as easily spare you the effort, and it will, if you let it.

There is a subtler problem. Theory has always grown out of practice. Most conductors started on an instrument, and structural engineering has been here before. For some thirty years frames were analysed with Hardy Cross's [moment distribution](https://cee.illinois.edu/about/history/history-excellence/hardy-cross) (the 1932 paper ran eight pages and drew 144 pages of discussion): by hand, balancing one joint after another until the moments settled, and learning along the way where moments want to go. The engineers trained that way went on to supervise the first finite element codes, and could spot a wrong moment diagram at a glance. 

In the far 1983, Bainbridge described the same pattern among the [ironies of automation](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8): automated plants monitored by former manual operators, _"riding on their skills, which later generations of operators cannot be expected to have."_ Today it is our turn: we learned our trade doing ourselves what agents now do for us. Once the practice is gone, theory no longer comes as a by-product of work. It has to be trained on purpose, like muscles in an office job.

<br> <br>

Plato taught in a gymnasium, the [Academy](https://iep.utm.edu/plato-academy/), just outside the walls of Athens; Aristotle chose another, the [Lyceum](https://iep.utm.edu/lyceum/). Both lent their names to schools (in Italy and Germany, the gymnasium itself became one), and for twenty-four centuries we have used those words for buildings full of classrooms. Now that agents are taking the work out of our hands, perhaps it is time to go back to the gym.
