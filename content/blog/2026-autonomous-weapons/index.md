> This time I really do not know what I am talking about. I do not understand the subject, I do not understand the technology behind it, and I do not work anywhere near the sector. Most posts on this blog grow out of what I deal with in my research, the things I work on and read about every day, and more of those are coming (a few are already lined up). This one is different. It touches subjects I do not handle, so I leave the facts to the sources I link, to the reading list at the end, and to the many others you can find out there. What follows is just what I have managed to extract from thoughts that have been circling in my head for a while: some reasonably structured, others still scattered and fragmented. If my usual rants belong at the bar, this one belongs at closing time.

That said, here are my two cents.

**Please, ban autonomous weapons.** Ban their development and any project that works on them, whether they run on agents, on whatever comes after agents, or on something that does not have a name yet.

## Joysticks and manicures

There is a scene in [Blade Runner 2049](https://letterboxd.com/film/blade-runner-2049/) that stayed with me. Luv, the replicant who does the dirty work for her maker, directs a drone strike on a group of scavengers while a manicurist paints her nails. When I first saw it, it looked like the kind of detail a director puts on screen to tell you how deep into dystopia you are. Today it looks more like a documentary.

We are already there. People sitting on the other side of the world wage war with a joystick, flying drones so high that nobody on the ground can even see them, and those drones kill with frightening precision. I will leave the news of the last few years, from more than one front, to you. If you read about Ukraine as much as I do, you know that unmanned systems are now everywhere, in the air, on land and at sea. Ukrainian naval drones have [sunk or damaged more than a dozen Russian warships](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/ukrainealert/ukraines-robot-army-will-be-crucial-in-2026-but-drones-cant-replace-infantry/), and a single ground robot with a machine gun reportedly held a front-line position for about six weeks. A Ukrainian brigade [took a fortified Russian position using nothing but aerial drones and ground robots](https://www.defensenews.com/unmanned/2026/04/24/ukraine-to-field-25000-ground-robots-in-push-to-replace-soldiers-for-frontline-logistics/), and Ukraine is now the first country with [a separate military branch built around unmanned warfare](https://united24media.com/defense-tech/no-tanks-no-troops-ukraine-sends-its-robot-army-through-kyiv-in-world-first-drone-parade-21926). Last August, instead of a military parade, ground robots rolled down Khreshchatyk while naval drones sailed the Dnipro.

But this is not what this post is about. In every one of those cases, somewhere, there is still a person who decides. They may be sitting in a dugout a few kilometres behind the front, or in an air-conditioned room on another continent, possibly getting a manicure, but the decision to kill still passes through a human head. What I am talking about is the next step: a machine that chooses on its own whom to strike.

That step is closer than it looks, and it is being pushed by the logic of the battlefield more than by anyone's ideology. Jamming cuts the radio link between the pilot and the drone, and there are two ways out. One is to keep the human connected through [a fibre-optic cable unspooling behind the drone](https://www.imeche.org/news/news-article/what-will-come-next-in-ukraine-s-drone-war). The other is to let the drone [finish the attack on its own](https://euromaidanpress.com/2025/05/29/new-footage-shows-ukraines-fpv-drone-bypasses-jamming-in-autonomous-strike/) once the link drops. Today this autonomy is narrow, limited to the final stretch of the attack and to a target a human has already locked. But each step in that direction makes perfect tactical sense, and the steps add up. SIPRI was already [mapping this creeping autonomy in weapon systems](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2017/other-publications/mapping-development-autonomy-weapon-systems) back in 2017. Add agents to the picture, models that plan, choose their tools and act without asking, and the last human in the chain starts to look like an optional component.

## War is not slavery (unfortunately)

For many years I held an idea borrowed from Gino Strada, the Italian war surgeon who founded Emergency. I heard him at a conference during my first years of high school: war can be abolished, the way slavery was. I never managed to find that talk online, but he repeated the idea many times, for instance [when he received the Right Livelihood Award in 2015](https://www.avvenire.it/opinioni/pagine/aboliamo-insieme-la-guerra-unutopia-da-realizzare-adesso) (in Italian). The argument is simple and powerful. For centuries humanity practised slavery, and it seemed normal, almost part of human nature. Then, not without resistance, it disappeared, at least in the forms we knew (the ILO still counts [some fifty million people in modern slavery](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/50-million-people-worldwide-modern-slavery-0), which is another story, and not a reassuring one).

I believed it for years, and I kept building on it. Then, hopefully not just because I am getting older, I started to see the differences. Slavery is a human institution. Violent conflict within the same species is not: it shows up all over the animal kingdom. The latest case I read about involves Argentine ants in southern California, where rival supercolonies have been fighting along their borders for generations. A [study led by UC San Diego](https://biology.ucsd.edu/about/news/article_113006.html) estimated that border skirmishes around a single colony north of San Diego killed at least fifteen million workers in six months (the same San Diego, incidentally, that Blade Runner 2049 turns into a landfill). I have my own idea of why this happens, but it lies well outside my field, and honestly I have not read enough to have even a complete picture of the phenomenon.

Either way, I no longer think we will stop waging war as a species. War has changed enormously from the American Civil War to today, and it will keep changing, but it seems it is here to stay.

If anything, this makes the question of *how* more important, not less. War stays, but some ways of fighting it can be taken off the table, and we have done it before: chemical weapons, anti-personnel mines, cluster munitions. Once, we even managed to do it in advance, and that story deserves a section of its own further down.

## Why not this one

These are the reasons that convinced me. Two came from reading around, two had been in my head for a while. None of them is new, by the way: UNIDIR, the UN's own disarmament research institute, has produced [fifteen in-depth studies](https://unidir.org/publication/unidir-on-lethal-autonomous-weapons) on autonomous weapons since 2013, and the academic debate is older than that.

### Nobody left to say no

On 26 September 1983, the Soviet early-warning system reported a missile launched from the United States, then several more. The duty officer, [Stanislav Petrov](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2026/09/1983-averting-potential-nuclear-war), judged it a false alarm and waited for a confirmation that never came. The cause turned out to be sunlight reflected by high-altitude clouds straight into the satellite's sensors. A system doing exactly what it was designed to do would have passed the alarm up the chain. A man with a bad feeling in his gut did not.

That is the first thing autonomy removes: the possibility that someone, at the last moment, says no. It is not only about false alarms. Even the worst dictator has to wonder whether his soldiers will actually fire on the crowd, and more than one regime has fallen on the day they did not. As [Tom Malinowski argued](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/tom-malinowski-responds-autonomous-lethal-systems) years ago, autonomous weapons would give tyrants something none of their predecessors ever had: an army that never refuses an order.

### Nobody left to sign

In my field, every structural design carries the signature of an engineer who answers for it if the building collapses. We would never accept a bridge designed by nobody. With autonomous weapons, we are about to accept deaths decided by nobody. When a machine kills the wrong people, who goes to court? The programmer who wrote the code years earlier? The manufacturer? The commander who switched it on without being able to predict what it would do? [Human Rights Watch warned back in 2015](https://www.hrw.org/news/2015/04/08/killer-robots-accountability-gap) that all of them could escape liability, under both criminal and civil law. The philosopher Robert Sparrow had already [made the point in 2007](https://robsparrow.com/wp-content/uploads/Killer-robots.pdf), long before anyone was talking about agents: if nobody can be held responsible for a death, deploying the weapon that caused it is itself unethical. Responsibility does not vanish all at once. It gets diluted along the chain, until there is nothing left.

### Not like the bomb

The comparison that comes naturally is with nuclear weapons, and it is exactly the wrong one. Nuclear deterrence is macabre but, so far, stable. Nuclear weapons are expensive and hard to build, they are in few hands, an attack can be attributed, and each side knows the other can destroy it. Everybody is held in place by the same pressure from every direction, and everybody understands, as the computer in [WarGames](https://letterboxd.com/film/wargames/) concluded that same year, that the only winning move is not to play.

Autonomous weapons have none of these properties. They are cheap, and software can be copied for free. They can end up in anyone's hands, including those who sign no treaties and have no territory to lose. An attack by a swarm of anonymous drones is hard to attribute. Above all, speed rewards whoever delegates more: if my system decides in milliseconds and yours waits for a human, I win, so you will remove your human too. And AI does not stay politely on its side of the fence: SIPRI has devoted [an entire report](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2020/policy-reports/artificial-intelligence-strategic-stability-and-nuclear-risk) to how its adoption by nuclear-armed states could affect strategic stability and nuclear risk. When two adversarial systems start reacting to each other faster than any person can follow, we already have an idea of how it can end. On 6 May 2010, automated trading systems interacting with each other helped [wipe almost ten percent off the Dow Jones in minutes](https://foreignpolicy.com/2018/09/12/a-million-mistakes-a-second-future-of-war). Markets bounced back within the hour, and regulators added circuit breakers. There is no bouncing back from a flash war.

There is no stable equilibrium here, only a race in which every player has good reasons to take people out of the loop. In this game, the one who does not win is humanity, on every side.

### No jihad, please

Frank Herbert imagined the end of this story sixty years ago. In the background of [Dune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dune_(novel)) (1965) lies the Butlerian Jihad, the crusade in which humanity destroyed its thinking machines and left behind a single commandment: "Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind." The prequels written decades later turned it into a war against killer robots. In Frank Herbert's original, the danger was subtler, and it sounds very familiar: people handed their thinking over to machines in the hope of being set free, and this only allowed other people, the ones who owned the machines, to enslave them. An army that never refuses an order is exactly that.

I do not want us to get there. I use the same family of technology every day, the [agents I have written about before](../2026-ecee-science-w-agents/), and I find it extraordinary. Precisely for this reason, I would rather see a targeted and boring ban now than a desperate and total rejection later. A protocol signed in Geneva is far better than a jihad.

### The strongest objection

The strongest objection I have read is that machines might be more precise than tired, frightened and angry soldiers, and that fewer civilians might die. If you want the best version of that side of the argument, there is a whole [Oxford University Press volume](https://www.oupcanada.com/catalog/9780197546048.html) whose authors set out to rebalance a debate they see as dominated by science-fiction framings. Maybe they are right about precision. But precision was never the problem: the drones I mentioned at the beginning are already frighteningly precise. The problem is who decides, who answers, and how many decisions per second we are willing to take out of human hands. Nor am I talking about the defensive systems that dozens of countries have used for years, [under human supervision](https://foreignpolicy.com/2018/09/12/a-million-mistakes-a-second-future-of-war), to shoot down incoming missiles and drones. I am talking about machines that choose whom to kill.

Verification will be hard, as [the negotiators themselves admit](https://www.janes.com/defence-intelligence-insights/defence-news/air/special-report-autonomous-weapons-talks-advance-but-verification-obstacles-remain). Chemical weapons are banned and have been used anyway. But whoever uses them knows they have crossed a line the whole world can see. A norm does not stop everyone; it makes those who break it visible.

## A month from now, in Geneva

There is a story I like a lot, and it belongs to the Red Cross. In the late 1980s, lasers designed to blind soldiers were getting ready to move from the laboratory to the production line. The issue was first raised at the International Conference of the Red Cross in 1986, with little success. Between 1989 and 1991 the ICRC convened a series of expert meetings to understand whether such weapons would really be built and whether a specific treaty was needed. In 1994 it went public, lobbying governments together with the National Societies, medical associations and organisations such as Human Rights Watch. On 13 October 1995, in Vienna, the first Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons adopted Protocol IV, which prohibits lasers designed to cause permanent blindness. Louise Doswald-Beck, a former head of the ICRC's legal division, [told the whole story](https://www.icrc.org/eng/resources/documents/article/other/57jn4y.htm) in the International Review of the Red Cross a few months later, and [looking back in 2022](https://disarmament.unoda.org/update/experts-reflect-on-the-origin-relevance-and-future-of-ccw-protocol-iv-on-blinding-laser-weapons/) she recalled that momentum for a ban grew precisely as industry was getting close to mass production. A weapon was taken off the table [before its use became widespread](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/advocacy-paper-key-opportunity-prevent-development-unacceptable-autonomous-weapons).

Same convention, same kind of meeting. From 16 to 20 November, the states parties meet in Geneva for the Seventh Review Conference, which will decide whether to [launch negotiations on a legally binding instrument](https://dig.watch/processes/gge-laws) on autonomous weapons. The first Review Conference banned a weapon before it spread. The seventh can do it again.

The Red Cross is still at it. Since 2021 its position has been clear: [prohibit autonomous weapons whose effects cannot be predicted or explained, and those designed or used against people, and strictly regulate all the others](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-position-autonomous-weapon-systems). On 5 October 2023, ICRC President Mirjana Spoljaric and UN Secretary-General António Guterres issued [a joint call](https://disarmament.unoda.org/en/spotlights/united-nations-secretary-general-and-president-international-committee-red-cross-call) to negotiate a new legally binding instrument and [conclude it by 2026](https://news.un.org/en/story/2023/10/1141922). They renewed it this summer, and in the meantime the General Assembly has adopted three resolutions in a row on the subject. The issue had first reached the UN back in 2013, with [a report by the Special Rapporteur Christof Heyns](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/755741).

I know, lately the UN seems to count for very little: a Security Council stuck on vetoes, wars it cannot stop, a budget that its own Secretary-General has described as [a race to bankruptcy](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/news/2025/10/111909/un-faces-race-bankruptcy-guterres-unveils-sharply-reduced-2026). But this is exactly the job it was built for: a table where even rivals can agree on what nobody should be allowed to do. And nobody can say the homework has not been done. SIPRI and the ICRC have worked out [practical measures of human control](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2020/new-sipri-and-icrc-report-identifies-necessary-controls-autonomous-weapons), on the design of the weapon, on the environment where it is used, and on the way people interact with it. The final text of the expert group was [significantly watered down](https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/09/07/un-talks-on-killer-robots-ends-with-calls-for-negotiations-growing), but it is enough to start from, and more than seventy states have [declared themselves ready to negotiate](https://futureoflife.org/ai-policy/government-leaders-call-for-treaty-negotiations-on-autonomous-weapons-for-the-first-time/).

Meanwhile, the line is being drawn somewhere else. Earlier this year, the Pentagon designated Anthropic [a supply chain risk](https://www.npr.org/2026/03/06/g-s1-112713/pentagon-labels-ai-company-anthropic-a-supply-chain-risk) after the company refused to drop its restrictions on the use of its models for mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons. OpenAI, on the other hand, [signed a deal](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/05/its-official-the-pentagon-has-labeled-anthropic-a-supply-chain-risk/) allowing the military to use its systems for all lawful purposes, with a contract that reportedly rules out directing autonomous weapons [only where law or policy requires human control](https://www.wilpf.org/ccw-report-vol-14-no-2-the-final-stretch-before-the-finishing-line/). Read that twice: even the red line in the contract points back to the law. If the law does not require a human, the clause is empty. Today, the boundary between a weapon and an autonomous weapon depends on a usage policy, a contract clause, the resolve of a single CEO. It is far too important to be left to any of them.

## Not a manifesto

This post is not a manifesto. It is not an expert opinion, nor a petition, nor a verdict on those who, like Ukraine, are defending themselves today with whatever works against an enemy that will not wait for Geneva. It is simply a political act, in the oldest sense of the word: a citizen saying what he thinks about something that concerns the society he lives in. I opened by saying that I know nothing about this subject, and that is precisely the point. You should not need to be an expert to have a say on who, or what, is allowed to decide to kill.

I no longer believe, as Gino Strada did, that we will abolish war. But I still believe we can decide how it may be fought: that behind every death there will be someone who decided it, who could have said no, and who will have to answer for it. Even Luv, between one coat of nail polish and the next, had to give the order herself. Let us keep at least that.

## Further reading

For anyone who wants to go further than my two cents, here is where I would start, roughly grouped by who wrote what.

**The international process**

- ICRC, [Position on autonomous weapon systems](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-position-autonomous-weapon-systems) (2021). The Red Cross proposal in a few pages: what to prohibit and what to regulate.
- ICRC, [A key opportunity to prevent the development of unacceptable autonomous weapons](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/advocacy-paper-key-opportunity-prevent-development-unacceptable-autonomous-weapons) (2026). The advocacy paper ahead of the Review Conference.
- UN Secretary-General and ICRC President, [Joint call on autonomous weapon systems](https://disarmament.unoda.org/en/spotlights/united-nations-secretary-general-and-president-international-committee-red-cross-call) (2023), with the [UN News coverage](https://news.un.org/en/story/2023/10/1141922).
- C. Heyns, [Report of the Special Rapporteur on extrajudicial, summary or arbitrary executions](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/755741), A/HRC/23/47 (2013). The report that brought the issue to the UN.
- Digital Watch, [GGE on lethal autonomous weapons systems](https://dig.watch/processes/gge-laws). A timeline of the CCW process, updated as it goes.

**Research institutes**

- SIPRI, V. Boulanin and M. Verbruggen, [Mapping the Development of Autonomy in Weapon Systems](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2017/other-publications/mapping-development-autonomy-weapon-systems) (2017).
- SIPRI and ICRC, [Limits on Autonomy in Weapon Systems: Identifying Practical Elements of Human Control](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2020/new-sipri-and-icrc-report-identifies-necessary-controls-autonomous-weapons) (2020).
- SIPRI, [Artificial Intelligence, Strategic Stability and Nuclear Risk](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2020/policy-reports/artificial-intelligence-strategic-stability-and-nuclear-risk) (2020).
- UNIDIR, [UNIDIR on Lethal Autonomous Weapons](https://unidir.org/publication/unidir-on-lethal-autonomous-weapons) (2021). A guide to almost a decade of UNIDIR studies, organised by topic.

**Academic**

- R. Sparrow, [Killer Robots](https://robsparrow.com/wp-content/uploads/Killer-robots.pdf), Journal of Applied Philosophy (2007). The classic paper on the responsibility gap.
- M. Taddeo, [The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence in Defence](https://academic.oup.com/book/58663), Oxford University Press (2024). An ethical framework for AI in defence, from the Oxford Internet Institute, with autonomous weapons among the case studies.
- [Lethal Autonomous Weapons: Re-Examining the Law and Ethics of Robotic Warfare](https://www.oupcanada.com/catalog/9780197546048.html), Oxford University Press (2021). An edited volume that deliberately pushes back on the dominant narrative: the best place to read the other side.
- L. Doswald-Beck, [New Protocol on Blinding Laser Weapons](https://www.icrc.org/eng/resources/documents/article/other/57jn4y.htm), International Review of the Red Cross (1996). The Red Cross story, told by someone who was there.

**Advocacy and commentary**

- Human Rights Watch and Harvard Law School, [Mind the Gap: The Lack of Accountability for Killer Robots](https://www.hrw.org/news/2015/04/08/killer-robots-accountability-gap) (2015).
- Human Rights Watch, [UN Talks on Killer Robots End with Calls for Negotiations Growing](https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/09/07/un-talks-on-killer-robots-ends-with-calls-for-negotiations-growing) (2026).
- [Stop Killer Robots](https://www.stopkillerrobots.org/), the coalition campaigning for a treaty.
- T. Malinowski, [Responds on Autonomous Lethal Systems](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/tom-malinowski-responds-autonomous-lethal-systems), Lawfare.

**The drone war in Ukraine**

- Atlantic Council, [Ukraine's robot army will be crucial in 2026 but drones can't replace infantry](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/ukrainealert/ukraines-robot-army-will-be-crucial-in-2026-but-drones-cant-replace-infantry/) (2026).
- Defense News, [Ukraine to field 25,000 ground robots in push to replace soldiers for frontline logistics](https://www.defensenews.com/unmanned/2026/04/24/ukraine-to-field-25000-ground-robots-in-push-to-replace-soldiers-for-frontline-logistics/) (2026).
- IMechE, [What will come next in Ukraine's drone war?](https://www.imeche.org/news/news-article/what-will-come-next-in-ukraine-s-drone-war)

**Fiction, for the evenings**
