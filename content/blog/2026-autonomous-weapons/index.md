Before we start: this has been, so far, the post that has cost me the most time and energy, between reading and trying to understand the problem. So here is a disclaimer, and please keep it in mind all the way through.

> I am not an expert on this subject. I do not understand the technology behind it, and I do not work anywhere near the sector. Most posts on this blog grow out of my research, from what I work on and read every day, and more of those are coming. This one does not. The facts belong to the sources I link and to the reading list at the end; what follows is only what I have managed to extract from thoughts that have been circling in my head for a while, some structured, others still fragmented.

That said, here are my two cents.

**Please, ban autonomous weapons.** Ban their development and any project that works on them, whether they run on agents, on whatever comes after agents, or on something that does not have a name yet.

There is a scene in [Blade Runner 2049](https://letterboxd.com/film/blade-runner-2049/) that stayed with me. Luv, the replicant who does the dirty work for her maker, directs a drone strike on a group of scavengers while [doing her nails](https://youtu.be/yWUMWChI6MM?si=f-3cHztzPVX0NBhd). When I first saw it, it looked like the kind of detail a director puts on screen to tell you how deep into dystopia you are. Today it looks more like a documentary.

We are already there. People on the other side of the world wage war with a joystick, flying drones so high that nobody on the ground can see them, and those drones kill with frightening precision. I will leave the news of the last few years, from more than one front, to you. In Ukraine alone, unmanned systems now operate [at sea](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2024/03/05/world/ukraine-sea-drones/), [on land](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/ukraine-robot-rescues-soldier-trapped-russian-occupied-territory-ground-drone) and [in the air](https://www.npr.org/2026/10/05/nx-s1-5925803/drones-buzz-overhead-troops-hunker-underground-in-ukraines-hellish-kill-zone).

But this is not what this post is about. Behind each of those drones there is still a person who decides, in a dugout or on another continent, potentially getting a manicure. Call it the boundary condition of this post: I am talking about the next step, a machine that chooses on its own whom, where, when and maybe why to strike.

<br>

For many years I held an idea borrowed from [an Italian surgeon](https://en.emergency.it/blog/gino-strada/to-gino-strada-in-loving-memory/) who spent his life bringing remarkable medical care to the victims of war. I heard it from him in person, at a conference during my first years of high school: war can be abolished, the way slavery was. The argument is simple and powerful. For centuries humanity practised slavery, and it seemed normal, almost part of human nature at the time. Then, not without resistance, it disappeared, at least in the forms we knew (the ILO still counts [some fifty million people in modern slavery](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/50-million-people-worldwide-modern-slavery-0), which is another story, and not a reassuring one).



I believed it for years, and I kept building and rebuilding on it. Then, hopefully not just because I am getting older, I started to see the differences. Slavery is a human institution. {{< side src="ants.jpg" pos="left" width="27%" link="https://biology.ucsd.edu/about/news/article_113006.html" caption="Border skirmish, southern California. Photo by Marc Dantzker, from [UC San Diego](https://biology.ucsd.edu/about/news/article_113006.html)." >}} Violent conflict within the same species is not: it shows up all over the animal kingdom. The latest case I read about involves [ant colonies close to San Diego](https://biology.ucsd.edu/about/news/article_113006.html), where rival supercolonies have been fighting along their borders for generations, and skirmishes around a single colony killed at least fifteen million of them in six months (the same San Diego, incidentally, that Blade Runner 2049 turns into a landfill). As for why, I have not read enough to know what science has to say about it.


Either way, I no longer think we will stop waging war as a species. War has changed enormously from the American Civil War to today, and it will keep changing, but it seems it is here to stay.
If anything, this makes the question of *how* more important, not less. War stays, but some ways of fighting it can be taken off the table, and we have done it before: chemical weapons, anti-personnel mines, cluster munitions. Once, we even managed to do it in advance, and that story deserves a section of its own further down.

## Why not this one

These are the reasons that convinced me. Two came from reading around, two had been in my head for a while. None of them is new, by the way: UNIDIR has produced [fifteen in-depth studies](https://unidir.org/publication/unidir-on-lethal-autonomous-weapons) on autonomous weapons since 2013, and the academic debate is older than that.

### Nobody left to say no

{{< side src="petrov.jpg" pos="right" width="30%" link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislav_Petrov" caption="Stanislav Petrov, the man who waited, in 2016 ([source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Stanislaw-jewgrafowitsch-petrow-2016.jpg))." >}}
At the end of September 1983, while my parents were getting married, the Soviet warning system reported a missile launched from the United States, then several more. The duty officer, [Stanislav Petrov](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2026/09/1983-averting-potential-nuclear-war), judged it a false alarm and waited for a confirmation that never came. The cause turned out to be sunlight reflected by high-altitude clouds straight into the satellite's sensors. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislav_Petrov) adds that it took a rare alignment between the sun, the clouds above North Dakota and the Molniya orbits of the satellites, which I am copying here in all honesty without the faintest idea of how it works. A system doing exactly what it was designed to do would have passed the alarm up the chain. A man with a bad feeling in his gut did not.



That is the first thing autonomy removes, the possibility that someone, at the last moment, says no. It is not only about false alarms. Even the worst dictator has to wonder whether his soldiers will actually fire on the crowd, and more than one regime has fallen on the day they did not. As [Human Rights Watch argued](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/tom-malinowski-responds-autonomous-lethal-systems) years ago, autonomous weapons would give tyrants something none of their predecessors ever had: an army that never refuses an order.

### Nobody left to sign

In my field, every structural design still carries the signature of an engineer who answers for it if the structure collapses. Would we accept a bridge designed autonomously and never checked by a human being? Maybe, one day but with autonomous weapons, however, we are about to accept something else entirely: deaths decided by nobody. When a machine kills the wrong people, who goes to court? The programmer who wrote the code years earlier? The manufacturer? The commander who switched it on without being able to predict what it would do? These are complicated, slippery questions, especially in fields of strategic importance, and I would count structural engineering among them. I am not sure I can do them justice yet, but sooner or later I will have to try.

[Human Rights Watch warned back in 2015](https://www.hrw.org/news/2015/04/08/killer-robots-accountability-gap) that all three could escape liability, under both criminal and civil law. The philosopher Robert Sparrow had already [made the point in 2007](https://robsparrow.com/wp-content/uploads/Killer-robots.pdf), long before anyone was talking about agents: if nobody can be held responsible for a death, deploying the weapon that caused it is itself unethical. Responsibility does not vanish all at once. It gets diluted along the chain, until there is nothing left. Military chains of command have always appreciated this: "I was only following orders" now comes with a software update.

### Not like the atomic bomb

The comparison that comes naturally is with nuclear weapons, and it is exactly the wrong one in my opinion. Nuclear deterrence is macabre but, so far, stable. Nuclear weapons are expensive and technically hard to build (I suppose, no idea), they are in few hands, an attack can be attributed, and each side knows the other can destroy it. Everybody is held in place by the same pressure from every direction, and everybody understands, as the computer in [WarGames](https://letterboxd.com/film/wargames/) concluded, that the only winning move is not to play.

Autonomous weapons have none of these properties. They are cheap, and software can be copied for free. They can end up in anyone's hands, including those who sign no treaties and have no causes to lose. An attack by a swarm of anonymous drones is hard to attribute. Above all, speed rewards whoever delegates more: if my system decides in milliseconds and yours waits for a human, I win, so you will remove your human too. And AI does not stay politely on its side of the fence: SIPRI has devoted [an entire report](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2020/policy-reports/artificial-intelligence-strategic-stability-and-nuclear-risk) to how its adoption by nuclear-armed states could affect strategic stability and nuclear risk. When two adversarial systems start reacting to each other faster than any person can follow, we already have an idea of how it can end. 

On 6 May 2010, automated trading systems interacting with each other helped [wipe almost ten percent off the Dow Jones in minutes](https://foreignpolicy.com/2018/09/12/a-million-mistakes-a-second-future-of-war). That was 2010, the year of the first iPad: now picture the same dynamics with today's models. Markets bounced back within the hour, and regulators added circuit breakers. There is no bouncing back from a flash war.

{{< side src="decision_times.png" pos="full" width="100%" link="decision_times.png" caption="How long it takes to decide, from a single high-frequency trade to more than a decade of UN talks. Indicative orders of magnitude on a logarithmic axis." >}}

There is no stable equilibrium here, only a race in which every player has good reasons to take people out of the loop. In this game, the one who does not win is humanity, on every side.

### No Butlerian Jihad, please

_"Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind."_ It could pass for a line from some AI governance guideline, but it is the only commandment humanity kept after the Butlerian Jihad, the holy war in which, in the background of [Dune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dune_(novel)), it destroyed every thinking machine it had. Frank Herbert imagined it sixty years ago, and the interesting part is not the war but its cause. The machines never rebelled: no uprising, no red eyes glowing in the dark. People simply handed their thinking over to machines, hoping to be set free, and in doing so allowed the few who owned the machines to enslave everyone else. Not killer robots, then, but a perfectly efficient chain of command with nobody left in it who could say no. An army that never refuses an order, sixty years early.

I do not want us to get there. I use the same family of technology every day, [the agents I have written about before](../2026-ecee-science-w-agents/) (a scientific paper on them is coming soon, you will find it on the main page), I find it extraordinary, and we are only at the beginning. Which is exactly why I would rather draw a thin line now, in a treaty, than wait for the day when the only line left to draw is a butlerian jihad.

### The strongest objection

The strongest objection I have read is that machines might be more precise than tired, frightened and angry soldiers, and that fewer civilians might die. If you want the best version of that side of the argument, there is a whole [Oxford University Press volume](https://www.oupcanada.com/catalog/9780197546048.html) whose authors set out to rebalance a debate they see as dominated by science-fiction framings. Maybe they are right about precision. But precision was never the problem: the problem is who decides, who answers, and how many decisions per second we are willing to take out of human hands. Nor am I talking about the defensive systems that dozens of countries have used for years, [under human supervision](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c1k2lmmjvzxo), to shoot down incoming missiles and drones. I am talking about machines that choose whom to kill.

Verification will be hard. Chemical weapons are banned, and they [have been used anyway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_chemical_warfare#Use_in_conflicts_after_World_War_II). But whoever uses them knows they have crossed a line the whole world can see. A norm does not stop everyone; it makes those who break it visible.

## A month from now, in Geneva

The story I like most belongs to the Red Cross. In the late 1980s, lasers designed to blind soldiers were about to leave the laboratory. The ICRC spent years gathering experts and lobbying governments, and in 1995 the first Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons [banned them](https://www.icrc.org/eng/resources/documents/article/other/57jn4y.htm) before their use [became widespread](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/advocacy-paper-key-opportunity-prevent-development-unacceptable-autonomous-weapons). From 16 to 20 November, the same convention holds its Seventh Review Conference in Geneva, and it will decide whether to [start negotiating a treaty on autonomous weapons](https://dig.watch/processes/gge-laws). The first one banned a weapon before it spread. The seventh can do it again.

Just outside the Palais des Nations, a wooden chair twelve metres tall has been standing on three legs since 1997, a reminder of what landmines leave behind. The chairs I worry about are of a different kind: intact, comfortable, and empty.

{{< side src="broken_chair.jpg" pos="right" width="35%" link="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broken_Chair" caption="Broken Chair, Place des Nations, Geneva ([source](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:BrokenChair.jpg))." >}}

The Red Cross knows [exactly what it wants](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-position-autonomous-weapon-systems): prohibit autonomous weapons that are unpredictable or designed to target people, and strictly regulate all the others. In 2023, together with the UN Secretary-General, it [asked states to conclude a treaty by 2026](https://news.un.org/en/story/2023/10/1141922). I know, lately the UN seems to count for very little, between vetoes, wars it cannot stop and _a budget_ [_racing towards bankruptcy_](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/news/2025/10/111909/un-faces-race-bankruptcy-guterres-unveils-sharply-reduced-2026). But this is exactly the job it was built for: a table where even rivals can agree on what nobody should be allowed to do. The homework [has been done](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2020/new-sipri-and-icrc-report-identifies-necessary-controls-autonomous-weapons), and more than seventy states are [ready to negotiate](https://futureoflife.org/ai-policy/government-leaders-call-for-treaty-negotiations-on-autonomous-weapons-for-the-first-time/).

Meanwhile, the line is being drawn somewhere else. Earlier this year, the Pentagon labelled Anthropic [a supply chain risk](https://www.npr.org/2026/03/06/g-s1-112713/pentagon-labels-ai-company-anthropic-a-supply-chain-risk) after the company refused to drop its restrictions on fully autonomous weapons and mass domestic surveillance, while OpenAI [signed a deal](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/05/its-official-the-pentagon-has-labeled-anthropic-a-supply-chain-risk/) for all lawful purposes, with a contract that reportedly rules out using its systems to direct autonomous weapons [only where law or policy requires human control](https://www.wilpf.org/ccw-report-vol-14-no-2-the-final-stretch-before-the-finishing-line/). Even the corporate red line points back to the law. Today the boundary depends on a usage policy, a contract clause and the resolve of a single CEO: far too important to be left to any of them. I have an idea of my own about this, but it deserves a post of its own: mixing it in here would only blur both. A first glimpse is at the bottom of [this article](../2026-ecee-science-w-agents/), in the third comment of the 21 October addendum.

## Not a manifesto


This post is not meant to be a manifesto (I would not be able to write one), it is simply a political act, in the oldest sense of the word: a citizen saying what he thinks about something that concerns the society he lives in. I know nothing about this subject, and that is precisely the point: you should not need to be an expert to have a say on who, or what, is allowed to decide to kill. My only contact with these tools comes from research, working with a few algorithms, glimpsing what they can do and wondering what else they could be used for. None of this makes me an enemy of technocracy. I believe in it, just not in the version you read about everywhere (but that is a story for another post, and it involves fish metaphor I developed some years ago).


<br>
Unlike Gino Strada, who never stopped believing it, I have lost the illusion that we will abolish war. But I still believe we can decide how it may be fought: that behind every death there will be someone who decided it, who could have said no, and who will have to answer for it. **Let us keep at least that: somebody in the chair, with a finger on the trigger.**

## Further reading

For anyone who wants to go further than my two cents, here is where I started. It is a list put together by someone pushing well outside his own field, so it is certainly not exhaustive: for a living, I do something else. Roughly grouped (by claude) by who wrote what.

**The international process**

- ICRC, [Frequently asked questions: Autonomous weapon systems](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/faq-autonomous-weapon-systems) (2026). The quickest introduction: what these weapons are, why they worry the Red Cross, and what it asks of states.
- ICRC, [Position on autonomous weapon systems](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-position-autonomous-weapon-systems) (2021). The Red Cross proposal in a few pages: what to prohibit and what to regulate.
- ICRC, [A key opportunity to prevent the development of unacceptable autonomous weapons](https://www.icrc.org/en/article/advocacy-paper-key-opportunity-prevent-development-unacceptable-autonomous-weapons) (2026). The advocacy paper ahead of the Review Conference.
- UN Secretary-General and ICRC President, [Joint call on autonomous weapon systems](https://disarmament.unoda.org/en/spotlights/united-nations-secretary-general-and-president-international-committee-red-cross-call) (2023), with the [UN News coverage](https://news.un.org/en/story/2023/10/1141922).

**Research institutes**

- SIPRI, V. Boulanin and M. Verbruggen, [Mapping the Development of Autonomy in Weapon Systems](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2017/other-publications/mapping-development-autonomy-weapon-systems) (2017).
- SIPRI and ICRC, [Limits on Autonomy in Weapon Systems: Identifying Practical Elements of Human Control](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2020/new-sipri-and-icrc-report-identifies-necessary-controls-autonomous-weapons) (2020).
- SIPRI, [Artificial Intelligence, Strategic Stability and Nuclear Risk](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2020/policy-reports/artificial-intelligence-strategic-stability-and-nuclear-risk) (2020).
- UNIDIR, [UNIDIR on Lethal Autonomous Weapons](https://unidir.org/publication/unidir-on-lethal-autonomous-weapons) (2021). A guide to almost a decade of UNIDIR studies, organised by topic.

**Academic**

- R. Sparrow, [Killer Robots](https://robsparrow.com/wp-content/uploads/Killer-robots.pdf), Journal of Applied Philosophy (2007). The classic paper on the responsibility gap.
- M. Taddeo, [The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence in Defence](https://academic.oup.com/book/58663), Oxford University Press (2024). An ethical framework for AI in defence, from the Oxford Internet Institute, with autonomous weapons among the case studies.
- [Lethal Autonomous Weapons: Re-Examining the Law and Ethics of Robotic Warfare](https://www.oupcanada.com/catalog/9780197546048.html), Oxford University Press (2021). An edited volume that deliberately pushes back on the dominant narrative: the best place to read the other side.
