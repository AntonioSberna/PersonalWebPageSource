This is not an introduction. The [homepage](https://antoniosberna.github.io/) already contains more than enough self-celebration for one website{{< note >}}Perhaps not for an academic's website :){{< /note >}}, and I see no reason to add to it. It is rather an attempt to answer a question I ask myself every time I open the editor: why this blog?

Not because my ideas are particularly clever. This is not a TED talk{{< note >}}Please do not ask me what I think of those.{{< /note >}}, and nobody is waiting for my take on anything. Not because of the audience either: who reads blogs anymore? The honest answer is more modest. Over the last couple of years I have found myself reading far more than I used to. Not books{{< note >}}My [StoryGraph](https://www.thestorygraph.com/) statistics are merciless on this point: no trend whatsoever.{{< /note >}}, but papers, preprints, documentation and reports, all work-related. The world, and my small corner of it, is changing fast enough that this reading cannot just pile up. It has to go somewhere, and writing seems to be its natural outlet. An editorial in *Nature Reviews Bioengineering* put it well: writing is not only a way to report results but a way to [think in a structured manner](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44222-025-00323-4), instead of letting the mind wander as it usually does.

I also have a broader hope, which goes beyond this blog. I would like the scientific community to write opinion pieces, essays and perspectives more often than it has in the recent past. Perhaps as it did in a more distant past, when correspondence and essays were part of how science was done, but for different reasons. As I argued in [a previous post](../2026-gym-for-theory/), the long and tedious groundwork of intellectual professions is increasingly carried out by agents. What is left to us is theory, and with it the time to argue about where things are going. In this sense, the present post is also a footnote to the one on [science in a world of agents](../2026-ecee-science-w-agents/).

That is roughly what this blog is for. What it is not is easier to say. It is not popular science: other people do that well, and it is their job. Nor is it a place where opinions meet and clash, which is why there is no comment section. In the age of LLMs, comment sections have become hard to keep alive. Paolo Attivissimo, who has moderated the comments on his blog one by one for years, recently [gave up and closed them](https://attivissimo.me/lo-spam-ha-vinto-chiudo-i-commenti-diretti-mandatemeli-via-mail/), observing that generating credible fake comments now takes far less effort than filtering them. (A pity, because I spent an unreasonable amount of time, and tokens, on the counter on the landing page, and I would love to know what you think of it. Let me know... no, you can't.) Apparently, this blog can only be defined by negation.

So here are two more negations, in the form of disclaimers. I am not an expert in anything, least of all in the topics I tend to write about here. My research is in civil engineering, and I do not feel entrusted with steering the fate of humanity. Nor is this blog an extension of my CV, as should be clear from the fact that the two are not linked. When the idea took shape, this was simply meant to be my free space: easy to build, and customizable exactly as I like.

And yes, easy also because of LLMs. They wrote much of the code behind these pages, with all the small features a site like this needs. They clean up my language, especially my English. They turn a vague idea into a cover image or an illustration, following a thousand odd whims about style and content, and they are patient enough not to tell me where to go at the twentieth iteration. (Not without limits, though. I have noticed that image models still tend to degrade quickly over a long session, as anyone who used ChatGPT in its early days will remember. With text the problem is gone; with images it is still very much there.)

What no LLM provides is an editorial plan, and there will not be one. This means peaks and long silences, which is fine, since I do not need people to read. The process is always the same. I have an idea, I write it down, I leave it there for a while, then I come back and revise it. If it holds up, I publish it. If it does not, it stays in revision. If too much time passes and I cannot fix it or bring it back to life, it moves to a folder of failed attempts and I forget about it. The day I have to scrape that folder for ideas, I will close everything down. I am not a journalist, and nobody pays me to fill a page.
