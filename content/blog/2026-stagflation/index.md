In the last addendum to [the elephant post](../2026-ecee-science-w-agents/) I compared the scientific literature to a night sky turning into a Milky Way, so crowded with stars that the constellations can no longer be read.{{< note >}}This should be the last post on the subject, unless the situation changes again or new thoughts on it come to me (both not unlikely). After this I move on: a few other ideas are waiting to take shape.{{< /note >}} That image was about finding one's way. But it hides a second problem, one an economist would spot before an astronomer: if stars can be produced at will, what is each of them worth? This post follows that question from the sky to the purse, asking what happens to the value of a paper, and of an idea, when we print them faster than anyone can read them.

## Printing money

Economists have a word for a currency that keeps losing value while the economy around it stands still: stagflation. If I remember correctly, it is what happened in the 1970s. In my view, academia is close to it, and it got there before LLMs arrived.

{{< side src="paid-in-papers.jpeg" pos="right" width="50%" caption="Paid in papers, change given in impact factor." >}}

Between 2016 and 2022, [Hanson and colleagues](https://direct.mit.edu/qss/article/5/4/823/124269/The-strain-on-scientific-publishing) counted about 47% more articles per year in Scopus and Web of Science, from roughly 1.9 to 2.8 million, while the number of practising scientists grew little, if at all. Among their metrics there is one they call, fittingly, *impact inflation*: impact factors rising across publishers, faster than any plausible rise in quality. Reading their paper, I found that the reason underneath has a name: Goodhart's law, when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.{{< note >}}Goodhart [formulated it in 1975](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-349-17295-5) about monetary targets, which suits this post rather well. The wording quoted here is [Marilyn Strathern's (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1062798700002660), and she was writing about British universities.{{< /note >}} Nobody needs to behave badly for this to happen. It is enough that everybody behaves rationally.


(I am part of the statistics. As I write, I am a breath away from 300 citations, with an h-index close to ten. [Hirsch](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0507655102) suggested that an h-index growing by about one point per year of activity marks a successful scientist,{{< note >}}His scale was calibrated on physicists: about one point per year for a successful scientist.{{< /note >}} so by his own yardstick I am an anomaly. In some corridors, though, I have been read as an anomaly in the other direction, and over the years I have collected every kind of encouragement to publish more, from the most academic to the most prosaic.)

{{< side src="wheel-of-fortune.jpeg" pos="left" width="50%" caption="Below 20%, the wheel does the reviewing." >}}

So much for the paper, the coin academia has traded in for generations. Its twin, the idea, is not faring any better, and a few days ago I came across an [article in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03476-3).{{< note >}}It is the piece that put the whole metaphor in my head.{{< /note >}} The ERC Starting Grant, one of the most coveted grants in Europe, received 7,710 proposals for the 2027 call, against 4,807 the year before, while the grants available dropped from 421 to at most 373 because of funding constraints. The success rate is expected to fall below 5%, the lowest since the very first call in 2007. AI is only part of the story: the eligibility window has widened, and more applications come from outside Europe. But writing a proposal has become much easier,{{< note >}}I touched on other sides of this [here](../2026-llm-privacy/).{{< /note >}} while the funds, and the capacity to actually carry out the research, have not grown with it. Neither have the people who evaluate it: in the words of ERC president Maria Leptin, quoted in the same piece, "there aren't enough referees". Lynn Kamerlin, who has [studied grant success rates](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44319-026-00779-4), told *Nature* that a healthy rate sits around 20%; below it, telling the best proposals apart is little more than a lottery.

More currency, the same goods, fewer buyers.

## What the oxen cannot do

Project the trend a few years forward. Agents will take care of papers from start to finish, from finding the topic to running the analyses to writing the text, as recent cases have already shown.{{< note >}}I wrote about one of them [from Berlin](../2026-ecee-science-w-agents/).{{< /note >}} Ideas will come the same way: give a language model a literature review, which by now it can compile on its own, and a handful of hints, perhaps the only human contribution left so far, steering it through a brainstorm, and it returns research directions that read well enough to be funded. The question then is what is left to read in a proposal, or in a paper. If a published paper no longer tells us much about the researcher who signs it, and neither does the idea behind it, the one that raised the funding through a grant, then counting papers, citations and h-indices to hire, promote or fund someone measures the wrong thing. The sooner we retire this kind of bibliometric evaluation, the better.


So what is a researcher for? The image that keeps coming back to me is a farmer before the industrial revolution, walking behind a plough pulled by oxen. The oxen supply the force: without them he could not move the ploughshare an inch, and would spend weeks sweating over the same field with a hand hoe. But they do not know which field to break, how deep the furrow should go, or when the soil is ready. He does, and he is the one who fits the *harness* that turns their strength into a furrow.

I would like to take one more step. Perhaps even the choice of the field will not stay with him for long. A swarm of agents could map the gaps in the literature, estimate the feasibility of each research path, and weigh the expected result against the resources needed to pursue it (the amount of tokens required) accounting honestly for the chance that nothing new comes out. At that point, why write grants at all? We could let agents pick the lines of research that promise the greatest benefit for humanity at the lowest cost. The field would be ploughed by tractors (perhaps self-driving ones).

{{< side src="beyond-the-field.png" pos="right" width="45%" caption="Some fields are not for oxen." >}}

And the farmer, freed from the field, could do what farmers' sons have always done when the land no longer needed them: leave. Set sail, climb the peaks, cross the polar ice, go to space. In research, that would mean keeping for humans the improbable and the impossible bets, the work of pure creativity. Not blue-sky research, which still has a horizon, but the dark space beyond it: endless, cold, and frightening.



**The work of research must shift from producing to directing, harness in hand, and from directing to exploring, where no harness reaches.** Those places are not for oxen. You go in person, with your own hands and the little you can carry. ## Fewer papers, other readers

Back down to earth: if that is the job, we should change the products too. For humans, we could return to what scientific writing looked like at the beginning of the twentieth century: a few pages explaining an idea to another person, with plenty of room for speculation, openly declared as such. Einstein called his 1905 paper on light quanta [*a heuristic point of view*](https://doi.org/10.1002/andp.19053220607): the speculation was in the title, not hidden in the discussion {AGGIUNGI SIDE NOTE: SPERO DI NON SBAGLIARE È COMPLETAMENTE FUORI DALLE MIE CAPACITÀ E CONOSCENZE DI BASE}. In 1912 Alfred Wegener proposed, in [a paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02202896) most geologists of his time dismissed, that the continents drift; half a century later it became plate tectonics. In 1950 Alan Turing opened his [essay on thinking machines](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433){{< note >}}A wonderful read. I have not gone back to it in a long time, and I should, soon.{{< /note >}} by replacing the question of whether machines can think with a game; we are still playing it.

For the agents, a separate product altogether: everything they need to use and check the work, data, code, models, results in a form a machine can verify. As I have found first-hand,{{< note >}}Some of it is in [These Are All English Words](../2026-on-thinking-differently-screen/).{{< /note >}} much of this can already be written in {AGGIUNGI: QUALCOSA CHE HA MOLTO DI} natural language, and these machine-facing documents will only grow more sophisticated and more precise. What matters is to keep them apart from the human ones. Today's paper sits awkwardly in between, half technical record for reproducibility, half interpretation for the reader, and fully serves neither. All the more now that one of the two halves is no longer handles by humans, splitting it in two would let each half do its job. And evaluation would look at whoever drives the oxen, or sails beyond the field, not at furrows the oxen dug on their own.{{< note >}}How language models could help with that, without simply grading other language models, deserves a post of its own.{Aggiugni prima quaclosa che ancora non mi è chiaro, dovrei leggere ancora molto sull'argomento}{{< /note >}}

The sooner we make these changes, the better. The alternative is to be swept along by something we cannot control, and then rush to repair the damage with improvised solutions: the history of disaster response is full of them.

<br>

For decades the rule was *publish or perish*. The numbers say that the currency it relied on is inflating: more papers than anyone can read, more proposals than anyone can fund, while money, people and referees stand still. Language models will only speed this up, because they make both the paper and the idea cheap to produce, and a measure of how much we produce stops measuring us. What they do not make cheap is direction: choosing the field, the depth of the furrow, the season. Nor do they make cheap the free time to get bored, to wander off and look into a strange new plant {SOSTITUIAMO QUESTA IMMAGINE CON UNA PIÙ FORTE, DI PREPARARE LO ZAINO O LE VELE}, a trend that today looks like a crazy road. That should be the direction of the researcher's work, and that is what evaluation should look at, while papers go back to being written for people and a separate set of products is built for machines.

Hanson and colleagues close by recommending CVs that highlight researchers' best work over total volume. It is a good start, and the choice ahead looks less like publish or perish and more like publish or plough. But ploughing may turn out to be only the first half of the job. Someone will still have to drive the oxen, and someone, free at last from counting stars, will have to look past the last one, into the dark part of the sky that no map has charted yet.
