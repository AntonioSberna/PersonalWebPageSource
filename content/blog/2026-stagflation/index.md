In the last addendum to [the elephant post](../2026-ecee-science-w-agents/) I compared the scientific literature to a night sky turning into a Milky Way, so crowded with stars that the constellations can no longer be read.{{< note >}}This should be the last post on the subject, unless the situation changes again {AGGIUNGI ANCHE CHE NON MI VENGANO ALTRI SPUNTI SULL'ARGOMENTO} (which, at the current pace, is not unlikely). After this I move on: a few other ideas are waiting to take shape.{{< /note >}} Here I want to take one step further, from the sky to the purse: what happens to the value of a paper, and of an idea, when we print them faster than anyone can read them.

## Printing money

Economists have a word for a currency that keeps losing value while the economy around it stands still: stagflation. Academia is close to it, and it got there before language models arrived.

Between 2016 and 2022, [Hanson and colleagues](https://direct.mit.edu/qss/article/5/4/823/124269/The-strain-on-scientific-publishing) counted about 47% more articles per year in Scopus and Web of Science, from roughly 1.9 to 2.8 million, while the number of practising scientists grew little, if at all. Among their metrics there is one they call, fittingly, *impact inflation*: impact factors rising across publishers, faster than any plausible rise in quality. The mechanism has a name, Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.{{< note >}}Goodhart formulated it in 1975 about monetary targets, which suits this post rather well. The wording quoted here is Marilyn Strathern's (1997), and she was writing about British universities.{{< /note >}} Nobody needs to behave badly for this to happen. It is enough that everybody behaves rationally.

{{< side src="paid-in-papers.jpg" pos="right" width="40%" caption="Paid in papers, change given in impact factor." >}}

(I am part of the statistics. As I write, I am a breath away from 300 citations, with an h-index close to ten. [Hirsch](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0507655102) suggested that an h-index growing by about one point per year of activity marks a successful scientist,{{< note >}}His full scale, calibrated on physicists: about one point per year for a successful scientist, two for an outstanding one, three for a "truly unique" individual.{{< /note >}} so by his own yardstick I am an anomaly. In some corridors, though, I have been read as an anomaly in the other direction, and over the years I have collected every kind of encouragement to publish more, from the most academic to the most prosaic.)

That was the paper. Now the idea. The ERC Starting Grant, one of the most coveted grants in Europe, received [7,710 proposals for the 2027 call, against 4,807 the year before](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03476-3), while the grants available dropped from 421 to at most 373 because of funding constraints. The success rate is expected to fall below 5%, the lowest since the very first call in 2007. AI is only part of the story: the eligibility window has widened, and more applications come from outside Europe. But writing a proposal has become much easier, and readers have not multiplied. In the words of the ERC president, "there aren't enough referees". Lynn Kamerlin, who studies grant success rates, puts a healthy rate at around 20%; below it, telling the best proposals apart is little more than a lottery.

{{< side src="wheel-of-fortune.jpg" pos="left" width="36%" caption="Below 20%, the wheel does the reviewing." >}}

More currency, the same goods, fewer buyers.

## What the oxen cannot do

Project the trend a few years forward. Papers will be written by agents, each batch more convincing than the last. Ideas will come the same way: give a language model a literature review and a handful of hints, and it returns research directions that read well enough to be funded. I recently wondered [who else reads a proposal](../2026-llm-privacy/) drafted with these tools; the next question is what is left to read in it. If the paper no longer measures the researcher, and neither does the idea, counting them is pointless, and the sooner we retire evaluation by bibliometric numbers, the better.

So what is a researcher for? The image that keeps coming back to me is a farmer before the industrial revolution, walking behind a plough pulled by oxen. The oxen supply the force: without them he could not move the ploughshare an inch. But they do not know which field to break, how deep the furrow should go, or when the soil is ready. He does, and he is the one who fitted the harness that turns their strength into a furrow. (Those who work with agents use the same word for the scaffolding of tools, instructions and checks built around a model. Farmers just got there first, by a few millennia.) **The work of research is shifting from producing to directing.**

The night sky had already said so. What Americans call the Big Dipper and the British the Plough, the Romans called *septentriones*, the seven plough oxen, and from them they named the north itself.{{< note >}}Varro and Aulus Gellius describe a yoked pair of oxen as a triangle, without saying where the third vertex is. [García Hernández](https://openpolar.no/Record/ftrecyt:oai:recyt.fecyt.es:article%2F96917) suggests it is the ploughman.{{< /note >}} Beside them stands Boötes, the ox-driver. The stars that point north were never meant to find it on their own.

## Fewer papers, other readers

If that is the job, the products change too. For humans, we could return to what scientific writing looked like at the beginning of the twentieth century: a few pages explaining an idea to another person, with room for speculation, openly declared as such. Einstein called his 1905 paper on light quanta [*a heuristic point of view*](https://doi.org/10.1002/andp.19053220607): the speculation was in the title, not hidden in the discussion. For the agents, everything they can use and check: data, code, models, results in a form a machine can verify. And evaluation would look at whoever drives the oxen, not at furrows the oxen dug on their own.{{< note >}}How language models could help with that, without simply grading other language models, deserves a post of its own.{{< /note >}}

## Coda

Hanson and colleagues close by recommending CVs that highlight researchers' best work over total volume. It is a good start. But in a sky this crowded, the question is no longer how many stars we have added. It is whether anyone still knows where north is, and who is driving the oxen to get there.
